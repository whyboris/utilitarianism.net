Utilitarianism is the view that one morally ought to always promote overall well-being _impartially_. According to it, the well-being of all welfare subjects is valued equally. You should not, at least in principle, privilege the well-being of your friends, families, and even _yourself_. So understood, the prioritization of self-interest (as opposed to general interest) will be the exact opposite of the spirit of utilitarianism.

Unfortunately, the most popular Chinese translation of utilitarianism in mainland China over ~130 years— “{{< chinese >}}” —may guide people to associate with that exact opposite. And this translation issue may have negative consequences that are worth worrying about. As such, an international group, formed around a panel at ISUS 18 in Macau, is asking whether the current translation should change.

More importantly, the group has found that the negative impact of the current translation may go beyond the circle of Chinese speakers. So, yes, this is relevant to you even if you are an English speaker, as we shall discuss later.

## Part One: The Problem in the Circle of Chinese Speakers

To begin, let’s explain how the Chinese word 功利主义 may look from a Chinese speaker’s perspective. To break it down, 主义 is "-ism" and 功利 is an ordinary, living Chinese word for the pursuit of reputation and gain. [Mainland dictionaries gloss it as 功名利禄，多含贬义](https://zdic.net/hant/%E5%8A%9F%E5%88%A9) — "fame and profit, usually pejorative." In many contexts, the word 功利主义 by itself is used to accuse someone of being self-centred, egotistic, or money-seeking. This is the default, everyday understanding of the word 功利主义.

Now the question is whether or not we should at least reconsider 功利主义 as a Chinese translation of utilitarianism in moral philosophy. The position of this article is that we should.

### 1.1 The worry of false negative association

First and the foremost, 功利主义 raises the worry of false negative association. The word itself suggests the exact opposite of what the original word means. In real-life communication, 功利主义 may mislead people to falsely make unnecessary negative association, blocking them from appreciating utilitarianism as a view that aims to promote welfare impartially. This may make it more difficult to promote utilitarianism in China.

Some may think that the worry of false negative association can be overcome by case-by-case or context-by-context clarification. For example, to prevent conceptual confusion, the speakers can simply remind the audiences to carefully distinguish two senses of 功利主义: one refers to the ordinary understanding, while the other refers to the ethical theory in philosophy. So, as they may argue, challenging the current translation is unnecessary.

Despite this, we believe that there is something deeper about the worry of false negative association that cannot be overcome simply by case-by-case or context-by-context clarification.

First, while context-by-context clarification may help, it seems better to eliminate the need for such clarification in the first place. The fact that clarification is required whenever utilitarianism is discussed or mentioned is by itself a barrier to Chinese people’s fair understanding of the theory. This barrier also increases the communicative cost: articles, lectures, and explanatory notes have to be delivered simply to address the misconception and confusion caused by this translation, such as this. The possibility to lower a barrier is not a sufficient reason for not removing it when possible.

Imagine a Chinese teacher explaining utilitarianism to beginners in philosophy. The teacher makes the disclaimer that the word 功利主义 here refers to the ethical theory that seeks to maximize welfare impartially, not the pursuit of self-interest. But sufficiently reflective and critical students may reasonably wonder why the theory is translated as 功利主义 if it does not mean 功利主义 in the ordinary Chinese sense. Questions like “does utilitarianism ultimately imply or endorse some form of self-centered pursuit?” may occur and it requires extra explanatory effort to address.

A similar problem may arise in the case of a donor who is interested in financially supporting philosophical research. If a project presents itself as research on 功利主义, the donor may hesitate because the Chinese name suggests something morally suspect and reputationally discrediting.

The takeaway is that the very presence of the word 功利主义 has already provoked negative connotation effects. One strong evidence for this is AI connotation. Any AI chatbot is able to provide a roughly correct definition of utilitarianism (or at least one that is not as bad as rendering it as “self-interested”): A simple prompt could test this. But according to Tse (2026)’s study, AI chatbots, despite knowing the true definition of utilitarianism, and also knowing how to apply the two senses of 功利主义 in different contexts, are still forming undeserved negative associations with the word 功利主义, and it seems to have even polluted the English word ‘utilitarianism’ (as discussed here [put url]). Third, the current translation leaves 功利主义 ambiguous in a unique way that a same word can mean two opposing ideas. This creates two related problems. First, it risks confusing both writers and the readers, especially when the ordinary and the philosophical senses of 功利主义 are mentioned or discussed side by side within the same work. Second, such an ambiguity of 功利主义 may be deliberately exploited. That is, some writers may take advantage of the negative association effects of 功利主义 to advance their own points. ### 1.3 功利主义 has been considered problematic by some Chinese scholars for years

Last but not least, we acknowledge that 功利主义 is considered misleading even by some Chinese scholars who use it to talk about utilitarianism. For them, 功利主义 is kept just for conventional reason, as 功利主义 has been popular for many years in China (citation). If they change the translation by themselves, some readers may feel confused. This shows that the ongoing usage of 功利主义 may just be a result of compromise. If changing it is possible, people may welcome the change.

One instructive case is Lü Shouwei's 《庸俗功利主义教育的精神哲学审视》 (2024). The writer spends its first third on Bentham and Mill, treats them sympathetically, and says outright that “功利主义的庸俗理解不仅不是功利主义的，而且在本质上是反功利主义的” — the vulgar reading of 功利主义 in Chinese is essentially anti-utilitarian. Yet the title, abstract, keywords and every index entry read ‘the vulgar reading of 功利主义 education. The disclaimer sits in section two; the title is what travels. An author who sees the exact problem of the current translation still has no other word to use.

## Part Two: How the Problem is Relevant to English Speakers

We have said at the outset that the Chinese translation issue is also relevant to English users. Here are the key reasons why this is the case:

1. Your Writings Might be Misunderstood by Chinese Speakers
2. You Might be Misled by Chinese Articles Translated into English
3. You should care about what happens in China/Chinese

### 2.1 Your writings might be misunderstood by Chinese speakers

While English people may not read philosophical text written in Chinese, Chinese people do read English work, and they may sometimes translate the work to Chinese, using translation models or AI chatbots. The translation of these articles will render “utilitarianism” as “功利主义”. Since a lot of lay people might feel a repugnance toward “功利主义”, it might cause them to less able of appreciating utilitarianism, or whatever idea is being supported or justified by utilitarianism. For instance, imagine the impact on a non-philosopher when they are told that Peter Singer advocates for animal liberation as a “功利主义者” (utilitarian).

Furthermore, as mentioned, AI chatbots are forming undeserved negative associations with the word 功利主义 even when knowing the true definition of utilitarianism, as well as how to distinguish it from the ordinary sense of 功利主义. And this seems to have even polluted the English word ‘utilitarianism’. It is therefore reasonable to worry that when translating articles that mention or discuss utilitarianism, translation models or AI chatbots might be prompted by the keyword “功利主义” to give an overall negative sentiment to the article it is asked to translate [URL link to an intro article to sentiment analysis in relation to LLM]

### 2.2 You might be misled by Chinese articles translated into English

This is a subtle point which you might never realize. The current translation creates a backward translation issue. To explain, consider three types of Chinese article.

First, there are enormous number of materials, academic ones and non-academic, using the terms “功利主义者” (the people), and “功利主义” (the theory), without intending to mean anything related to utilitarianism in ethics. Such materials, if they are ever translated, are usually rendering “功利主义者” as “utilitarian”, and “功利主义” as “utilitarianism”. ### 2.3 You should care about what happens in China/Chinese

If you are a utilitarian (or an Effective Altruist), or sympathetic to it, shouldn't you also care about what happens in China/Chinese? Since the translation issue might have real impact there, such as how well utilitarianism could be taught, popularized, and adopted there.

Even if you are not, shouldn't you at least care about whether utilitarianism is being discussed, critiqued, and rejected in a fair way, on the basis of good arguments; instead of reasons such as that it sounds bad?

## Part Three: Responding to Objections That Undermine or Rejects the Issue

Some people might raise objections to undermine, or even reject, the claim that this translation is causing major issues. This section responds to those objections.

Note that this section is not responding to objections against the case for changing the translation; this will be covered here [URL] instead.

### 3.1 There is a similar problem in English

This objection says that the translation issue in Chinese is not particularly worrying because English has the similar problem. In English, the word ‘utilitarian’ also carries negative meanings in daily conversation. If this is not a big issue for English scholars, the same may go for Chinese.

There are two responses to this objection. First and foremost, the fact that there is a similar problem in the English word "utilitarianism" does not mean that there is not a problem in its translation, "功利主义”.

Also, even though the word "utilitarian" in English also carries negative meanings in daily conversation, the problem is much more severe in Chinese, for two reasons.

First, the extent of the negative meaning in English is less severe. For instance, in a daily, non-philosophical conversation, if one’s taste for fashion is called “utilitarian”, it roughly means that one focuses on one’s clothing’s practical functions, but not at all on the aesthetics of it. In English, the meaning is never as bad as "short-sighted", let alone "egoistic", which are the ordinary meaning of 功利主义 in Chinese.

Second, while some non-philosophers do use the word "utilitarian" as a negative word, the word "utilitarianism" is mostly used by those who study philosophy, politics, or law, and rarely used as a negative word in daily conversations; and even when it is, the speaker probably does intend to criticise the true utilitarianism (whether their criticism is plausible will be another question). Also, the meaning of the word "utility" varies across different contexts, such as those related to bills, public affairs, politics, law, and philosophy. But most of them are not negative.

However, in Chinese, a similar problem exists for all of "功利” (“utility), “功利主义者”/”功利主义的” (utilitarians/utilitarian-adv), and “功利主义” (utilitarianism). This means that, in a daily, non-philosophical conversation, all these could mean “you are a short-sighted, material seeking egoist!”

- “You are so 功利!”
- “You heart is filled with 功利!”
- “You are a 功利主义者!”
- “You are too 功利主义!”

### 3.2 The problem is long enough to cease to be a problem anymore

Some versions of this argument we have heard, such as that “Those who should know would know”, or “This problem is so old that scholars, including utilitarians, are already used to it”, are just a disguise for defeatism. But the most steelmanned version of this argument is probably that, since the problem has existed for so long, there are already good ways of getting around it. But we believe that this is empirically untrue. Even without a well-designed survey, all utilitarians we know in China, including the ones who raised this objection, said that non-scholars rarely know about the true utilitarianism. And when non-scholars utter, in daily conversation, the words "功利主义”, they almost certainly mean the negative sense (“short-sighted”, “egoistic”, “material seeking”).

To make things worse, there are scholars who, intentionally or not, use "功利主义” to mean both the negative meanings, and (true) “utilitarianism”, in the same article. This kind of articles will continue to cause confusion, and undeserved negative attitudes toward utilitarianism, as long as “功利主义” is still the standard Chinese translation of “utilitarianism”.

### 3.3. Only non-scholars are misled; it therefore does not matter

There are two responses to this objection. First, it is untrue that only non-scholars are misled by, or misuse, “功利主义”. We have evidence that scholars from politics, educational studies, politics, and even philosophy [何怀宏文章] are either deliberately misusing the word “功利主义”, or are themselves confused by the ambiguity of this Chinese term (or both).

Second, even if it is (or will become) true that only non-scholars are misled by, or misuse, “功利主义”, there are nonetheless actual consequences for the general, “non-scholar” population to be misled or confused by the translation.

Recall the examples of philosophy beginner and potential donor. Imagine how it sounds if the donors tell their board members, family, friends, or the general public that they have donated $1M to the study and promotion of “功利主义”. It might even be reasonable for them to wonder: “If the ethical theory of (true) “功利主义” really isn’t about “short-sightedness” and “egoism”, or might even claim to be against these values, why was “功利主义” chosen as the translation? Why did they not change it? Perhaps the current translation remains for some reasons. Utilitarianism may have something to do, indirectly or secretly, with these values.”

In any case, it seems that the confusion and undeserved negative impression toward "功利主义” is clearly one of the biggest obstacles against the popularization and fair critique and discussions of utilitarianism.

### 3.4 Clarifications alone will do!

We already addressed this objection in section 1. But since this objection is frequently raised, a more detailed and vivid response will be offered here.

First, generally, giving clarification does not always work. All utilitarians would know that it is hard enough to clarify what utilitarianism implies, or what it is, even when the interlocutor clearly knows that the subject is philosophical utilitarianism, as an ethical theory.

Secondly, even when it works, giving clarification is usually costly. Consider the time and energy it took to write, deliver, and make readers to understand this article, with all those qualifiers such as “true” utilitarianism or “the ordinary sense” of 功利主义? Don’t you realize that we would not need to write this article if not for the issue? Furthermore, the mission of clarification looks more difficult when we, again, consider those articles that employ two senses of "功利主义” in the same article, using parentheses after each mention of “功利主义” to indicate which sense they are using. It is unnecessarily time-consuming for both the writers and the readers, and possibly still a bit confusing to the readers.

Thirdly, giving clarifications requires us to make a claim that might strike some as incredible and unbelievable claim: an ethical theory that is translated as “功利主义” is actually a theory against short-signtedness and egoism.

Additionally, even if it could somehow remove all confusions and misunderstanding on the conceptual level, it is unlikely that giving clarifications would remove all the negative feelings and impressions the current translation creates. Furthermore, a study by one of us, TYF, found that even AIs are affected: Large Language Models (LLMs) reveal negative connotations such as "egoistic", "short-signtedness", and "material seeking" in their word embeddings of "功利主义”.
