I’m a utilitarian philosopher who doesn’t speak Chinese. Yet when I visited China two years ago, I was disturbed to learn, from several of the leading Chinese scholars working in ethics, that the standard translation of “utilitarianism” in mainland China - _gōnglì zhǔyì_ - is a term that refers to the pursuit of material gain, and not even for everyone, but primarily for oneself. The root of the word, gōnglì, without the zhǔyì, which translates “ism,” is used to accuse someone of being self-centred, short-sighted, egotistic, or money-seeking.

In other words, the connotations of “utilitarianism,” when translated into Chinese are close to the exact opposite of utilitarianism. The translation gives no sense that when utilitarians say that an action is right if it produces the best consequences, the consequences to be taken into account are those for everyone, and even for all sentient beings, with similar interests being weighed equally.

Obviously, as a utilitarian, I was concerned that the negative overtones of the Chinese translation of “utilitarianism,” would prejudice Chinese students, and ordinary people, against taking utilitarianism seriously, as a living ethical theory, and one that many people take as a guide to how they should live and act. I believe that we should all strive to bring about the highest possible level of wellbeing for all our fellow-humans, and for non-human animals as well, and I would like that view to gain a fair hearing in China, in the hope that then more Chinese people will also promote the wellbeing of others. But with the term mistranslated in the way that it is, utilitarianism must be starting off at a huge disadvantage, compared to other ethical views that are translated more accurately.

Even if I were an advocate for a different normative theory – whether it was a virtue-based ethical theory, or a rule-based one, a form of Kantianism or contractualism, a natural law ethics or a form of intuitionism, I would want a level playing-field for the discussion of ethical theories in China. There is value in thinking clearly and reasoning well, in general and about ethics in particular. That requires an accurate understanding of the main ethical theories. Gaining more followers for one’s favorite ethical theory because one of the leading alternatives has been grotesquely misunderstood would be a hollow victory.

Although the most serious consequences of an inaccurate translation will be experienced in China, they are not limited to China. Many Chinese thinkers are now interacting with thinkers and scholars from other countries, and having an influence all over the world, in many different fields, including philosophy and ethics. Even when these interactions are not among philosophers, but among those involved in public policy, or in areas like medicine, where ethical decisions have to be made in daily professional practice, ethical questions will be discussed. If we want these discussions to be fruitful, it is important that Chinese and non-Chinese people have the same understanding of what the words we use mean.

There can also be problems when readers who do not understand Chinese use translations from that language that refer to utilitarianism and when foreign-language articles that use the word “utilitarianism” or its equivalent are translated into Chinese. In the former case it may be unclear whether the meaning of the original Chinese was a reference to utilitarianism, or to egoism, self-seeking and short-sightedness. In the latter case, if Chinese people who are not trained in philosophy and are reading a foreign-language work translated into Chinese come across the term gōnglì zhǔyì they are most likely to read this as egoism, self-seeking and short-sightedness. Even those trained in philosophy will be uncertain about the meaning in the original language.

Of course, how a word is best translated into Chinese is not for me, an outsider, and a person who does not speak Chinese, to say. But I know that even some Chinese scholars and translators who use gōnglì zhǔyì are dissatisfied with it, but continue to use it in their own work because it has for many years been the most commonly used translation and they do not know how to change it. Indeed, for any individual scholar to change a translation is very difficult, and to attempt to do so would be to risk misunderstanding. Therefore I wish to encourage Chinese speakers to discuss the issue, and consider what alternatives might be available, and see if there is a consensus for a change.

Therefore, when the International Society for Utilitarian Studies held its 2026 conference in Macau, I was pleased to be invited to chair a plenary session on the translation of “utilitarianism” in both China and Japan. (The translations have similar connotations in both those countries.) The large audience was virtually unanimous in support of taking an initiative to consider the possibility of changing the current translation, and I am happy to play a role in bringing about a discussion of that change.

That leaves open the question of how we should proceed. I suggest that the first step is to have an open conversationabout the desirability of a change, and what other terms might be available to replace _gōnglì zhǔyi_. This conversation could take place in both Chinese and English: in Chinese through the WeChat site 功利主义研究, and in English through the website [utilitarianism.net](https://utilitarianism.net). We will see whether a consensus for change emerges, and canvas opinions about the available alternative translations

I welcome alternative suggestions, and I and others concerned with this issue would greatly appreciate your contributions. My own view is that the present translation of “utilitarianism” in the Chinese language is an important barrier to fair and accurate discussions of ethical theories in China, and through China’s growing international influence, throughout the world.
